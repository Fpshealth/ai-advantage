# Writing rules

Apply these rules to the page after you write the red thread. Part 1 applies in every language.
Part 2 applies only when the page is in English. When a rule makes a sentence worse, fix the
sentence another way or leave it alone.

## Part 1: every language

### Words

- Cut every word that does no work. "In order to" is "to". "It is important to note that" is nothing.
- Use the short, everyday word: "use", not "utilize". "Help", not "facilitate".
- Use the real name of a file, field, program or button, not a synonym or a description of it.
- Call each thing by one name everywhere. Three names for one thing teach the reader three things.
- Give each word one meaning. If "check" means inspect, do not also use it for stop.
- No AI vocabulary: crucial, delve, enhance, foster, landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase,
  testament, underscore, vibrant, seamless. Use the plain word.
- No fancy ways to say "is": "serves as", "stands as", "boasts". Say "is" or "has".
- No metaphor nouns where a concrete word exists: north star, flywheel, bedrock, paradigm, vector
  (way), wedge (add), endgame (last phase).
- No adverbs that prop up a weak verb: "significantly improves" becomes the measured number.

### Sentences

- One thought per sentence. Split instructions longer than about 20 words and other sentences
  longer than about 25.
- Mix sentence lengths. A short sentence lands a point. A longer one carries a fact with its
  condition.
- Active voice. Name who acts: "the shop system sends the file", not "the file is sent".
- Talk to the reader as "you", in the present tense.
- Write instructions as commands: "Open the file." Put the condition first: "To delete the order,
  click Delete." One instruction per sentence.
- Put the common case first, exceptions after.
- Make every "it", "this" and "they" point at one obvious thing. Repeat the noun when in doubt.
- Keep "only" and "not" next to the word they change.
- Break up long noun strings: "the supplier price list import check" becomes "the check that
  imports the supplier price list".
- Do not drop verbs or articles. Write whole sentences, not arrows or symbol-speak.

### Claims

- Say what a thing does, not how it feels. Name the mechanism or the number.
- If a sentence could appear unchanged in another company's document, it says nothing. Cut it.
- Name the source of a claim, or delete the claim. No "experts say".
- No hedge chains: "could potentially possibly" becomes "may".
- No "not just X, but Y". State the point.
- Do not force ideas into groups of three. Use the real number.
- No generic endings ("the future looks bright"). End on a fact or a next step.
- Say what you make of a trade-off instead of listing pros and cons.

### Punctuation and layout

- No dashes as connectors. End the sentence or use a comma.
- Colons only before a list or an example, not in the middle of a sentence.
- Use periods, not semicolons.
- Bold only UI elements and rare key terms, not every name.
- Headings in sentence case. No emojis in headings or bullets.
- Numbered lists for sequences, bullets for everything else. Introduce a list with a full sentence.
- Link text says where the link goes. Never "click here".
- No chatbot phrases ("I hope this helps!", "Great question!").

## Part 2: English pages only

- Follow ASD-STE100 for instructions: one approved meaning per word, no "-ing" forms where a
  simpler form works.
- Keep "the" and "a": "Remove backup file" reads two ways, "Remove the backup file" reads one.
- No slashes ("a, b, or both", not "a/b"). No "(s)" plurals. No Latin abbreviations (e.g., i.e., etc.).
- Use straight quotes and serial commas.
